# PFAS Water Technology Landscape — Executive Brief

## Strategic question

Which PFAS water-treatment technologies show the strongest combination of scientific momentum, patent activity and evidence of practical deployment?

## Key message

The PFAS treatment landscape is moving away from single-step removal toward integrated treatment architectures.

The clearest emerging pattern is:

**capture → concentrate → regenerate where possible → destroy**

Established capture technologies such as activated carbon and ion exchange remain central because they can treat large water volumes effectively.

Their main limitation is that they transfer PFAS into another medium.

Destructive technologies such as electrochemical oxidation, supercritical water oxidation and plasma therefore become strategically important when applied to smaller, concentrated PFAS streams.

## Evidence base

The current landscape integrates:

- 658 scientific publications from 2018–2026;
- technology-specific patent analyses;
- institutional and collaboration signals;
- manually validated pilot, field and full-scale evidence;
- cross-technology science–patent comparison.

The project is exploratory and reproducible rather than exhaustive.

## Technology signals

### Activated carbon

Activated carbon shows the strongest combination of maturity, deployment evidence and patent breadth among the capture technologies examined in detail.

Key signals:

- strong scientific activity;
- confirmed full-scale drinking-water treatment;
- 25 included patent families;
- innovation in modified carbon, regeneration, treatment trains and deployable systems.

**Strategic role:** mature front-end capture technology with continuing innovation around regeneration and system integration.

### Ion exchange

Ion exchange shows strong scientific relevance and a more focused patent profile.

Key signals:

- 53 scientific publications in the current corpus;
- pilot and field evidence;
- 17 included patent families;
- innovation in selective and functionalized resins, regeneration and capture-and-destroy configurations.

**Strategic role:** selective PFAS capture and concentration, particularly valuable as part of integrated treatment trains.

### Electrochemical oxidation

Electrochemical oxidation shows a particularly strong science-to-application signal among destructive approaches.

Key signals:

- sustained recent scientific growth;
- pilot and field-demonstration evidence;
- 15 retained patent families;
- substantial recent patent priority activity;
- academic and commercial participation.

**Strategic role:** promising downstream destruction technology for concentrated PFAS streams.

### Supercritical water oxidation

SCWO has a smaller scientific base but unusually strong application signals.

Battelle shows a coherent progression from aqueous treatment studies to field demonstration and site-specific deployment.

**Strategic role:** high-priority destruction pathway for concentrated PFAS wastes and difficult residual matrices.

## Strategic implications

The strongest opportunity is not a single universal treatment technology.

Future differentiation is likely to depend on the ability to combine:

- high-throughput capture;
- selective concentration;
- media regeneration;
- destruction of concentrated PFAS residuals.

This favours integrated treatment-system design over isolated technology substitution.

## Organizations to monitor

Several organizations show coherent science-to-application signals:

- Battelle — supercritical water oxidation;
- University of Georgia — electrochemical oxidation and integrated treatment;
- University of Surrey and Arcadis — sonolysis;
- Rice University — photocatalytic capture and degradation;
- Colorado School of Mines, Arizona State University and CDM Smith — applied PFAS treatment networks.

These should be treated as monitoring candidates rather than definitive market leaders.

## Priority monitoring areas

The highest-priority areas for continued technology intelligence are:

- electrochemical oxidation;
- supercritical water oxidation;
- regenerable activated-carbon systems;
- selective and regenerable ion-exchange systems;
- integrated capture-and-destroy treatment trains.

## Bottom line

PFAS treatment innovation is increasingly about system architecture rather than one technology replacing another.

The most credible near-term pathway combines mature capture technologies with increasingly deployable destruction technologies.
